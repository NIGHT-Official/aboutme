Case study · Desktop app · October 2026

From a design document to a product on sale, in two days.

SnapShelf is a paid Windows and Linux app for people who sell online. One person directed several AI tools to build it. This is what was built, how long it took, and what each of us got wrong.

What it does

Drop in a folder of product photos, tick the platforms you sell on (Etsy, eBay, Shopify, Instagram, Gumroad), see a live preview of each platform's version, and export one correctly sized, renamed set per platform. Originals are never changed. It has a free version, a $4.99 license, and its own license server.

The design and research before the first line of code were done by another AI tool and were not timed. Whether anyone wants the product is not yet known, and nothing here claims otherwise.

Who did what

  • I chose the product, approved the design, agreed the screen layout from a sketch, designed the look, set the price and terms, set up the business and payment accounts, and made every decision between milestones.
  • A research assistant proposed the idea, wrote the first design, and later checked its own facts and the market.
  • A coding assistant wrote the code, the tests and the documents, reviewed other tools' work, and built the license server.
  • A design assistant turned my design direction into the app's finished look.

What went wrong, and what caught it

This is the part I would want to read as a client. Every item was written down when it happened.

  • The research was wrong in places. When the same tool was asked to verify its own design, 7 of 33 researched facts needed correcting, including a platform's size limits. Caught before any code depended on them.
  • The premise was checked last. The idea rested on the claim that no such tool existed on Windows. A market check, done only after ten milestones were built, found several small ones. The plan was finished as designed, the claim was removed everywhere, and the lesson recorded: "who else already does this?" costs an hour and belongs before the design.
  • A "verification" that verified nothing. A second AI marked the market check "VERIFIED". Its only sources were our own documents. The habit that catches this: read the source list before the conclusion.
  • A crash about one run in six appeared only when the tests were run several times in a row. A passing test suite had not been shown to be reliable.
  • A design decision that only failed when seen working. Watermarks landed in the empty padding around a photo, where a white one vanished. Found by looking at a picture of the real window, not by a test.
  • The payment provider changed a day after the code for it was written. The risk had been flagged, so the provider sat behind a small interface. The rules, the screens and most of the tests survived; a license server had to be designed that was never in the plan.
  • A free hosting plan that was not free for this. Reading its terms before building showed it allowed personal use only. The server moved to another host before a line was written.
  • What passing tests could not see. The first real call to the deployed license server failed twice, for two unrelated reasons. Either would have stopped every Windows customer from activating a key they had paid for. Both were found in minutes, because the first purchase was made in a sandbox before any customer existed.
  • My assistant's own slips are in the record too: a size reported wrong for a day because links were counted as copies, a commit that swept in unapproved work, a push made without reading a failing test.

What I would do the same

  • Agree the screen from a sketch first. Nothing had to be rearranged.
  • One milestone at a time, each with an exit test, and my go-ahead between them.
  • Try every package on a machine that did not build it. It found two faults the building machine could not have.
  • Do the first real purchase in a sandbox.
  • Keep the plan steady when surprising news arrives, park the new ideas, and review them once the planned work is done.

What I would do differently

  • Check the market before the design, not after the build.
  • Open the store before writing code for it. Ten minutes would have shown the provider had changed.
  • Treat any AI research as a claim until a second source, or a person, has read the original.
